## What it is

The Brown Davis 87L long range tank (part **TL300R1**) is a **replacement
sub-tank** for the LandCruiser 300 Series. Rather than adding a tank, it
**replaces the factory 30-litre sub-tank** on the passenger side with an
87-litre unit — so you gain capacity without giving up your spare wheel or
plumbing in an awkward second filler.

Brown Davis has been building long range tanks and underbody protection in
Australia since 1978, and this is a purpose-made fit for the 300, not a
universal tank made to suit. (There's also a larger **150L auxiliary**
option in the range if you want even more, but the 87L replacement is the
clean, no-compromise upgrade for most tourers.)

## Build quality and fitment

This is where the Brown Davis earns its reputation, and it's worth setting up
the article on the strength of the engineering before we get to the numbers:

- **Steel construction.** It's made from **2mm aluminised steel** — strong,
  repairable, and the material you want under a heavy touring 300 that sees
  corrugations and the odd rock.
- **Factory mounting points.** It bolts up to the **existing sub-tank
  mounts** and uses the vehicle's **factory filler point and fuel supply** —
  so it's a genuinely **seamless integration**, not a kit that fights the car.
- **Internal baffling / swirl pot.** Internal baffles control fuel slosh over
  rough terrain, and the swirl-pot design keeps fuel around the pickup so you
  can use right down to the last few litres on a side-slope or climb.
- **Protective finish.** A durable powder-coated finish fights corrosion and
  abrasion.
- **Clean install.** A straightforward **~2-hour swap** of the mid-mount
  sub-tank, **no spare-tyre removal**, and **no modifications** to the
  vehicle required.

In short: it goes in like Toyota could have fitted it, and it's built tougher
than the factory unit.

## The numbers: capacity and range

| | Factory | With Brown Davis 87L |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main tank | 80 L | 80 L |
| Sub-tank | 30 L | **87 L** |
| **Total** | **110 L** | **167 L** |
| Net gain | — | **+57 L** |

On Toyota's quoted **11.6 L/100km**, that lifts theoretical range from
roughly **~950 km to ~1,440 km** — and in the real touring world, where a
loaded, tyred-up 300 towing or on sand drinks a lot more than 11.6, those
extra 57 litres are the difference between *making* the next fuel stop
comfortably and sweating on it.

## Why carry the extra fuel — the real advantages

This is the heart of the article. The value isn't just "a bigger number" —
it's three concrete things:

### 1. You buy fuel where it's cheap, not where you're desperate

The single most underrated benefit. Out in remote Australia, roadhouse diesel
can be **$2.80–$3.00+ a litre** — and when you're low, you pay it, because you
have no choice. With 167 litres on board you carry enough range to **roll
past the expensive stops** and fill where it's more reasonable — say **$2.20 a
litre**. It's still not cheap, but in *relative* terms you're choosing the
price instead of the price choosing you.

The point isn't that fuel becomes cheap — it's that **carrying the fuel with
you turns price-gouging stops into optional ones.**

### 2. Peace of mind

There's a real, quiet value in knowing the fuel is **on board**. No mental
arithmetic at every settlement about whether you'll make it; no detouring to a
servo "just in case"; no jerry cans sloshing on the roof rack raising your
centre of gravity and your blood pressure. The extra range converts a source
of low-grade trip stress into a non-issue.

### 3. Genuine reach

Fifty-seven extra litres is **access** — the legs to take the longer, better
track, to sit out a closure or wait for a tide, to run the air-con and the
fridge and the winch without watching the needle. For a vehicle built to go
remote, fuel range is the enabler for everything else.

## Cost of the tank vs the value it returns

Let's be straight about the economics, because that's what makes the
positives credible.

The tank is around **$1,450**. If you look at it purely as **fuel-price
arbitrage**, here's the rough maths on the 57 litres you can now carry:

- Buying 57 L at **$2.20** instead of **$2.80** saves about **$0.60/L → ~$34**
  on that fill.
- At **$3.00** vs **$2.20**, it's about **$0.80/L → ~$46** saved on that fill.

So a remote trip where you'd otherwise have been forced into one or two
gouging stops claws back **tens of dollars at a time**. It won't pay itself
off in a weekend — on arbitrage alone you're talking dozens of remote fills —
but that framing **misses the point**, and the article should say so plainly:

> The Brown Davis isn't bought to **save money on fuel**. It's bought to buy
> **range, flexibility and peace of mind** — and the fuel-price saving is a
> steady bonus that chips away at the cost every single remote trip.

Add in that it's a **steel, factory-fit, repairable** tank that will outlast
many vehicles and can move to your next 300, and the value case is strong.

## The one downside: the factory fuel gauge

In the interest of a fair review, there's one genuine quirk — and only one
worth talking about.

Because the vehicle's ECU has the **original tank volume calibrated into it**,
and the new tank holds far more fuel without that reference data changing, the
**factory fuel gauge and distance-to-empty stop reading linearly.** Important
nuance: **the gauge still works** — it's not broken — it just **stays "full"
for longer** and the needle no longer maps evenly to how much fuel is actually
left.

In practice, on a 167 L total, it behaves roughly like this (approximate, and
specific to the vehicle — your 300 will settle into its own pattern):

| Factory gauge shows | Fuel actually remaining | What it really is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full | ~167 L | Full |
| **¾** | **~84 L** | about **half** the real capacity |
| **½** | **~50 L** | about **a third** of the real capacity |
| Empty | low | Empty |

So "three-quarters of a tank" is really about **half your fuel gone**, and
"half a tank" is really down to about a **third left**. The distance-to-empty
readout, which leans on that same calibration, is similarly optimistic.

None of this is a fault in the tank — it's a side effect of fitting far more
fuel than Toyota's gauge was calibrated for. **Once you learn the pattern it's
a non-issue**, and there's a clean fix below.

## FAQ

**Does the Brown Davis 87L tank replace or add to the factory tank?**
It **replaces** the factory 30 L sub-tank with an 87 L unit. The 80 L main
tank stays, so total capacity goes from 110 L to **167 L**.

**Do I lose my spare wheel?**
No — the swap doesn't require spare-tyre removal.

**Will my fuel gauge still work?**
Yes, but **not linearly**. It reads full for longer and the needle no longer
maps evenly to fuel remaining, because the ECU is calibrated to the original
volume. A ScanGauge programmed to 167 L restores an accurate
remaining-fuel/range readout.

**How much extra range do I get?**
The extra 57 L adds roughly **500 km** of theoretical range at Toyota's quoted
economy — more meaningful still when you're loaded, towing or on sand and
drinking more than the brochure figure.

**Is it worth the money?**
If you travel remote, yes — but think of it as buying **range, flexibility and
peace of mind**, with steady fuel-price savings on top, rather than a device
that pays for itself purely on cheaper fuel.

## Verdict

For a touring LandCruiser 300, the Brown Davis 87L replacement sub-tank is an
easy thumbs-up. It's a tough, steel, factory-fit upgrade that nearly doubles
your usable range and hands you control over **where and when** you buy fuel —
which, out in remote Australia at $3 a litre, is worth a great deal. The only
catch is a factory fuel gauge that no longer reads linearly, and that's a
known quirk with a clean fix: program a ScanGauge to the true 167-litre
capacity and let it do the maths.

**Rating: a standout long-range upgrade for any remote-touring 300 Series.**
